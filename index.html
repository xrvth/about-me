01 / Profile

Nice to meet you

Computer science engineering graduate, 4+ years in tech across web maintenance, QA engineering and automation. Until recently on Comscore's AI enabling team.

I've been around computers since I was 3, starting on Kubuntu, because that's what was in the house. At 8 I started opening game files to see what was inside and ended up making small mods for The Sims 2 and 3. By ten I was building websites and blogs, and I learned most of my HTML and CSS the way a lot of people my age did - which is to say by breaking Tumblr themes until they worked for me. The plan, obviously, was to build Baymax. I did not build Baymax, on account of being genuinely terrible at physics. I did eventually start building a wellbeing companion that reads how you write, which is a way to go for me.

I came to testing from programming and design rather than the other way round, and the two turned out to feed each other. QA is where I learned to troubleshoot properly: I find my own bugs before I call something finished, I think about the edge cases while I'm still designing, and I write more precisely than I did when I only shipped and patched features. Learning to break things carefully made me better at building them.

So my day-to-day is Python, shell and JavaScript, internal tools built from scratch and interfaces designed with UX in mind, with a tester's habits underneath all of it.

Lately I have been mostly building AI agents. A Claude-based QA assistant that reads Jira tickets and writes structured QA reviews and Zephyr test cases, and a Claude + MCP + Playwright system that turns a bug ticket into a reproducible browser test on its own. I care about what those cost to run and their performanceas much as what they produce: teaching the Playwright one to persist its generated code instead of re-prompting for every rerun took repeat runs from about 700k tokens to zero. Cheaper, faster, and a lot less water burned for the same answer.

Where I think AI belongs

I have been building agents as my job and I'm particular about where they go. They earn their keep on tedious, repetitive work: reading a hundred tickets, running the same check for the thousandth time. That's the job I want them doing, and they're genuinely good at it.

They don't belong in art. Illustration, design, music, writing that's meant to be someone's: that's expression, and the entire point of expression is that a person did it. I don't use generative AI for any of it, and it isn't a position I'm looking to be talked out of.

Even inside automation I'd rather use less of it than more. A lot of what gets called AI automation is an made up deterministic script, and it usually ends up slower, pricier and less predictable than the plain script would have been. The best decision I made on my Playwright agent was working out which steps never needed a model and taking it out of the loop: repeat runs went from about 700k tokens to zero, and got roughly ten times faster. The automation should reach for a model when the task actually needs judgement.

Personal

Baimo

Pythonscikit-learnNLPStatisticsClaude

A wellbeing companion that reads how you write, only says what it can prove, and admits when it doesn't know. This is the Baymax attempt, finally.

  • A lexicon reader scores free text on three axes, handling negation, intensity, contrast and phrases, so "not stressed" reads calm and "a bit tired" differs from "utterly exhausted".
  • Because the reading is independent of the sliders, it can notice you rated the day a 7 then wrote about it like it was a 3, then says so as an observation.
  • No claim escapes without passing a significance test and a minimum effect size, so it stays quiet for the first week instead of inventing insight from three Mondays.
  • Energy forecast is a calibrated scikit-learn classifier split by person.
  • Reports ROC AUC 0.700 instead of a 0.500 random-guess baseline, and prints the reliability table. Tomorrow is hard to predict and 0.70 is a "fair" performance.
  • Crisis language overrides the tone entirely and it stops being a mood tracker.

Personal

Tender

PythonMCPSQLiteOpen-MeteoClaude

An MCP server that reasons about houseplant care. Most plant apps are a watering timer; this one models why a plant may need water on a given day, based on mutliple factors.

  • Watering intervals are derived. A snake plant's textbook 21 days becomes 43 in an 18cm pot, low light, January dormancy and a heated flat, with every adjustment listed in plain language.
  • Indoor humidity comes from physics: the Magnus–Tetens saturation curve over live forecast data.
  • That distinction lets the diagnostic engine separate "your air is dry" from "you are underwatering" - two problems with near-identical symptoms, but opposite fixes.
  • Diagnosis scores every cause on symptom fit and thirteen circumstantial signals from that specific plant's history, then returns a discriminator: a physical check that tells the rival causes apart.
  • Carries a biological aging rule, so one yellow bottom leaf on a healthy plant comes back as nothing is wrong, which is the right answer more often than the alarming ones.

Personal

ShelfCheck

PythonSteam APISteamSpyClaude

Will you actually play it, or will it just sit there? Name a game on Steam and it measures the candidate against the games you already own, then tells you your fair price: the number at which it would be worth it to you, on your own historical numbers.

  • Weighs four components (value, follow-through, taste match, community) with community deliberately the smallest input, because the question is whether you will play it.
  • Projects playtime from the games you own that are most similar, weighted by similarity squared so the closest match instead of being averaged.
  • Gathers evidence on both sides and lets the against-column override the score outright. Without that, a good-value game with six unplayed lookalikes still scores well and the whole thing becomes an elaborate way of saying "yes, buy it".
  • Four named overrides can fire, including downgrading a buy when there isn't enough data to tell someone to spend money.
